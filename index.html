Sash windows

Original sashes are usually better timber than anything you can buy today.

Victorian and Georgian sashes were made from slow-grown heartwood. It is denser and more durable than modern softwood. Which is why a hundred-year-old window is often worth saving and a twenty-year-old one is not.

  • Rotten sections spliced out and replaced in matching timber
  • Cords, weights and pulleys replaced so they open properly again
  • Brush-pile draught-proofing fitted into the existing frame
  • Slim double glazing where the frame and the planners allow it

Straight answers

Repair or replace? The questions that decide it

We would rather talk you out of the wrong job than sell you it.

Is it cheaper to repair a sash window or replace it?

Usually to repair — often considerably. Rot is normally confined to the cill and the bottom rail, and those can be cut out and spliced. Replacement means a new frame, new glazing, making good inside and out, and losing the original timber, which on an older house is better than anything you can buy today.

Can you put double glazing into an original sash?

Often yes. Slim double-glazed units can go into many existing sashes, though it depends on the depth of the rebate and, if the building is listed, on whether consent will be granted. We will tell you honestly whether yours is a candidate.

Do I need permission in a conservation area?

Frequently. Conservation areas commonly restrict materials, glazing and the appearance of replacements, and listed buildings need consent even for like-for-like. Draught-proofing and repair are usually permitted where replacement is not, which is another reason restoration is worth looking at first.

How long does a sash restoration take?

A single window is typically a day or two depending on the repair. A whole house is planned so you are not left with open holes — we work through them in sections.

Do you work outside Dunstable?

Yes. We cover Bedfordshire and Hertfordshire and travel further for larger jobs and period property. Ring and ask — if it is too far to do properly we will say so.
